# Action Cycle

## Core Principle

Fractured uses **narrative-driven, freeform action flow** instead of rigid turn order. Play moves through **Crisis Frames**—scenes where danger, time pressure, or moral weight shifts the action.

## No Initiative, No Turn Order

### How It Works
- Each player acts when it makes sense narratively
- If an action could fail, trigger risk, or cost something, the GM calls for a roll
- No counting rounds or tracking whose turn it is
- The GM escalates tension through fiction or a Threat Clock when players hesitate or fail

### Why This Works
- Focuses on story and character, not mechanics
- Rewards creative thinking and bold choices
- Allows for cinematic, flowing action
- Prevents "waiting for your turn" syndrome
- Enables genuine surprise and chaos

## Crisis Frames

A **Crisis Frame** is a scene with:
- **Stakes**: Something that matters is at risk
- **Pressure**: Time, danger, or moral weight forces action
- **Agency**: Players must decide and act
- **Consequences**: Outcomes shape the story

### Types of Crisis Frames

**Combat Frame**
- Immediate physical danger
- Active threats (infected, hostiles, environment)
- Violence is likely
- Life or death stakes

**Social Frame**
- Interpersonal conflict
- Negotiations or persuasion
- Trust being tested
- Relationships at stake

**Survival Frame**
- Environmental challenges
- Resource scarcity
- Navigation or shelter
- Physical endurance tested

**Moral Frame**
- Ethical dilemmas
- Red Line moments
- Value conflicts
- Identity at stake

## Action Flow

### 1. GM Frames the Scene
Establish:
- **Where** you are
- **What** is happening
- **Who** is present
- **Why** it matters

**Example:**
"You're in a collapsed pharmacy. Infected scratch at the blocked door behind you. Sarah's bleeding out. You have minutes. What do you do?"

### 2. Players Declare Actions
Players state what they want to do:
- **Bold actions**: "I'm going to stabilize Sarah while covering the door"
- **Creative solutions**: "I'll use the shelves to reinforce the barricade"
- **Character moments**: "I tell Marcus this is his fault—he led us here"

The GM listens and asks clarifying questions:
- "How are you doing that?"
- "What does that look like?"
- "Are you sure?"

### 3. GM Determines Risks
For each action, the GM decides:
- **Automatic success**: No roll needed, it just works
- **Risky action**: Roll for it, failure has consequences
- **Impossible**: Can't be done this way, try something else

### 4. Resolve Actions
- **Automatic successes**: Narrate the outcome, move on
- **Risky actions**: Call for rolls, interpret results
- **Failed actions**: Make a GM move, escalate tension

### 5. GM Responds
After player actions resolve:
- Describe how the world responds
- Introduce new complications
- Escalate existing threats
- Create new choices

### 6. Repeat
Continue until the Crisis Frame resolves:
- Danger is overcome
- Stakes shift
- Scene naturally concludes
- Transition to new frame

## Threat Clocks

**Threat Clocks** track escalating danger in a scene.

### Structure
A clock has 4 or 6 segments:
- **4 segments**: Fast-moving threats
- **6 segments**: Slower-building danger

### Advancing the Clock
Add a segment when:
- Players fail a roll
- Players hesitate or stall
- Players take noisy/desperate action
- Time passes narratively
- The GM makes a move

### When the Clock Fills
**The threat manifests:**
- Infected breach the barricade
- Structure collapses
- Hostile reinforcements arrive
- Betrayal is revealed
- Panic spreads
- Time runs out

### Using Multiple Clocks
Track different threats simultaneously:
- **Breach Clock**: Infected breaking through
- **Bleeding Clock**: Ally dying
- **Discovery Clock**: Hostiles searching for you

Players must manage multiple pressures.

### Visible vs Hidden Clocks
- **Visible**: Players see the clock, tension builds transparently
- **Hidden**: GM tracks secretly, danger feels uncertain

Choose based on desired tension.

## Scene Types

### Action Scenes
Fast-paced, high stakes, physical danger.

**Structure:**
1. Establish immediate threat
2. Players respond quickly
3. Escalate with each action
4. Resolve decisively

**GM Moves:**
- Advance threat clocks aggressively
- Make dangers immediate and visceral
- Force split-second decisions
- Show consequences immediately

### Social Scenes
Interpersonal tension, negotiation, conflict.

**Structure:**
1. Establish social stakes
2. Players navigate relationships
3. Escalate emotional pressure
4. Force revelations or decisions

**GM Moves:**
- Introduce conflicting loyalties
- Reveal uncomfortable truths
- Force public choices
- Trigger emotional wounds

### Exploration Scenes
Discovery, navigation, scavenging.

**Structure:**
1. Establish location and risks
2. Players investigate and search
3. Introduce complications
4. Reward discovery, exact costs

**GM Moves:**
- Reveal environmental dangers
- Create navigation choices
- Make resources costly
- Introduce unexpected encounters

### Downtime Scenes
Recovery, reflection, preparation.

**Structure:**
1. Establish safe space
2. Players process and plan
3. Develop relationships
4. Prepare for next crisis

**GM Moves:**
- Allow genuine recovery
- Enable character moments
- Plant seeds for future tension
- Show passage of time

## Pacing

### Varying Intensity
Mix scene types for rhythm:
- **High intensity**: Action, crisis, survival
- **Medium intensity**: Social conflict, exploration
- **Low intensity**: Downtime, reflection, preparation

**Example session flow:**
1. Open with action (infected attack)
2. Cool down with social scene (aftermath, blame)
3. Build tension with exploration (scavenging run)
4. Explode with moral crisis (Red Line moment)
5. Resolve with downtime (processing, planning)

### Managing Momentum
**Building tension:**
- Shorten description
- Speed up pacing
- Increase stakes
- Advance clocks faster
- Give less time for planning

**Releasing tension:**
- Allow longer description
- Let players breathe
- Provide small victories
- Create safe moments
- Enable character interaction

### Scene Length
Let scenes breathe, but don't drag:
- **Action scenes**: 5-15 minutes
- **Social scenes**: 10-20 minutes
- **Exploration scenes**: 15-30 minutes
- **Downtime scenes**: 10-30 minutes

Cut scenes when:
- Stakes are resolved
- Energy dips
- Next logical scene beckons
- Players need variety

## GM Principles

### Don't Plan Scenes, Frame Situations
Instead of scripted scenes:
- Present pressure
- Provide stakes
- Let players respond
- Follow the fiction

### Ask Questions
- "What do you do?"
- "How does that work?"
- "What does that look like?"
- "Are you sure?"
- "What are you hoping for?"

### Telegraph Danger
Make risks clear before calling for rolls:
- "This is dangerous—if you fail, infected break through"
- "You can try, but if it goes wrong, you'll lose medical supplies"
- "She's watching you closely—this could break trust"

### Make Failure Interesting
Never make failure a dead end:
- Failure escalates situation
- Failure creates new choices
- Failure reveals information
- Failure triggers consequences
- Failure advances the story

### Honor Player Agency
- Let bold plans work (if they roll well)
- Allow creative solutions
- Respect character choices
- Make consequences follow naturally from actions

### Escalate, Don't Repeat
When tension builds:
- Each complication should be worse
- Each threat clock segment means something
- Each failed roll changes the situation
- Never stall—always move forward

## Example Crisis Frame

**Setup:**
> "You're trapped in a basement. The entrance collapsed—you'll need Engineering rolls to clear it. But Maria is having a panic attack, hyperventilating in the corner. And you hear scratching above you—something knows you're here. What do you do?"

**Player Actions:**
- **Alex**: "I go to Maria, try to calm her down."
  - GM: Roll Diplomacy. Mixed success: she calms but you lose time.
- **Taylor**: "I start moving debris from the collapse."
  - GM: Roll Engineering. Failure: you shift something wrong—it collapses further. Advance Breach Clock.
- **Jordan**: "I move quietly toward the scratching sound with my weapon ready."
  - GM: Roll Survival to move silently. Success: you position yourself well.

**GM Response:**
> "Maria's breathing slows—she nods at you, Alex. But Taylor, as you shift that beam, more debris cascades down. The opening is now smaller. And Jordan—you see cracks in the ceiling. Dust falls. The scratching stops. Silence. Then: a wet, clicking sound directly above you. A full threat clock segment. What do you do now?"

**Resolution:**
Continue until:
- They escape the basement (success)
- They're overrun (failure—new frame: desperate fight)
- They find alternative route (creative success)
- Scene reaches natural conclusion

## Integration with Other Systems

### With Traits and Flaws
- **Traits** activate during Crisis Frames to provide edges
- **Flaws** trigger under pressure to complicate actions
- Crisis Frames test Crisis Profiles

### With Resources
- Crisis Frames consume resources (ammo, medical, utility)
- Failed actions may cost additional resources
- Resource depletion creates new Crisis Frames

### With Red Lines
- Moral Crisis Frames test Red Lines
- Red Line moments pause the action cycle
- Crossing a Red Line changes all future Crisis Frames

### With Threat Clocks
- Clocks run throughout Crisis Frames
- Multiple clocks create layered tension
- Filled clocks spawn new Crisis Frames

## Troubleshooting

### "Players are talking forever"
- Introduce time pressure
- Advance a threat clock
- Have something happen
- Ask pointed questions

### "Everything feels too easy"
- Add threat clocks
- Increase complications on mixed successes
- Make failures harder
- Introduce conflicting pressures

### "Everything feels too hard"
- Allow automatic successes more often
- Give +1 bonuses more liberally
- Create small victories
- Provide downtime scenes

### "I don't know what happens next"
- Look at threat clocks
- Check Crisis Profiles
- Trigger a consequence from earlier
- Ask the players what they're worried about
- Frame a new scene

### "The pacing feels off"
- Cut scenes earlier
- Vary scene types
- Take breaks between intense scenes
- Let victories feel earned
- Don't fear quiet moments

## The Golden Rule

**Follow the fiction.**

If the narrative says the infected break through, they break through. If the story demands a quiet moment, provide it. If a player's bold plan deserves to work, let it work.

The action cycle serves the story—not the other way around.
